How do cyclists’ street-environment preferences vary within a day? Evidence from dockless bike-sharing route choices in Shanghai

Master’s Thesis · Tongji University · 2023–2026 · Advisor: Prof. Lan Wang

Highlights

  1. Revealed distinct within-day shifts in cyclists’ route preferences across morning peak, off-peak daytime, evening peak, and nighttime.
  2. Identified eye-level streetscape characteristics as important route-choice factors alongside conventional transport and land-use attributes.
  3. Developed data-driven choice sets from observed GPS trajectories, improving behavioural plausibility while reducing reliance on algorithm-generated routes.

Abstract

Route choice analysis provides direct evidence of cyclists' street-environment preferences by comparing chosen and alternative routes between the same origin–destination pair. Traffic volumes, street activity, and lighting conditions change substantially across the day and may therefore alter how cyclists value the same street environment. Yet how such preferences vary within a day remains poorly understood.

Using 1.3 million dockless bike-sharing trajectories from Shanghai, we constructed data-driven choice sets and estimated Path Size Logit models for 9,249 origin–destination pairs and 91,878 trips across four daily periods (morning peak, off-peak daytime, evening peak, and nighttime), incorporating eye-level streetscape indicators derived from street-view imagery.

The overall model revealed cyclists' general preferences, with eye-level streetscape indicators, including green view index and traffic-light visibility, showing significant associations alongside transport and land-use characteristics. Temporal patterns were also distinct. At both peaks, transport-related characteristics such as bicycle-lane share dominated, while cyclists also avoided land uses with time-specific concentrated activity, such as office areas in the morning peak, suggesting stronger efficiency concerns. During off-peak daytime, transport-related associations weakened while greenery preferences strengthened, suggesting a shift towards cycling comfort. At night, fewer characteristics were significant overall, but streetscape characteristics potentially related to perceived safety became salient, including higher traffic-light visibility and lower spatial enclosure.

These findings support differentiated priorities for bicycle-friendly street planning, such as prioritising bicycle-lane provision along high-demand peak-period corridors and improving traffic-light visibility and spatial openness along nighttime corridors.
